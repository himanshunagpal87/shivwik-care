## What is frozen shoulder?

The capsule around the shoulder joint becomes inflamed and tight, so the arm cannot be lifted or turned fully, whether you move it yourself or someone else moves it. It is more common in people with diabetes and between the ages of about 40 and 60. Other shoulder problems can look similar, so an assessment is important.

## What are the stages?

- **Painful stage**: pain builds up, often worse at night, and movement starts to reduce.
- **Stiff stage**: pain may ease, but the shoulder is clearly restricted.
- **Recovery stage**: movement slowly returns.

The stages overlap and their length differs from person to person.

## How does physiotherapy help at each stage?

- **Painful stage**: pain-relief measures, comfortable positions for sleep, and gentle movement within comfort. Forcing the shoulder at this stage can make it worse.
- **Stiff stage**: joint mobilisation, stretching and a regular home programme.
- **Recovery stage**: strengthening and return to full use of the arm.

## What can I do at home?

Use the arm for light daily tasks within comfort, do the specific stretches you are taught regularly, and avoid sudden, forceful movements. Heat before stretching helps some people.

## When should I see a doctor first?

See a doctor if the shoulder pain started after a fall or injury, if the shoulder is hot, red or swollen, if you have fever, or if arm pain comes with chest pain or breathlessness. If you have diabetes, keeping it well controlled with your doctor's help is part of looking after the shoulder.

## Questions patients ask

Q: How long does a frozen shoulder take to recover?
A: It varies widely and can be slow. We cannot give a fixed time. Regular, stage-appropriate exercise and follow-up help you keep as much movement as possible while it settles.

Q: Should I push through the pain when stretching?
A: A mild stretch discomfort is acceptable. Sharp or lasting pain is a sign to ease off. Your physiotherapist will show you how far to go at your stage.

Q: Is an injection or surgery needed?
A: Some people are offered an injection or other procedures by their orthopaedic doctor. That is a medical decision. Physiotherapy is usually part of care whichever route is taken.
